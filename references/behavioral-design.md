# Behavioral design by product task

Use this reference when a brief calls for a buying decision, product setup, a recurring goal, or discovery. Start with the user's task and the part they struggle with. Pick a pattern that helps at that point. Research on these effects can inform a design hypothesis. Check the result in the actual product before claiming an improvement.

## Product pages, pricing, and checkout

| Pattern | When it helps | What to build |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price anchoring and framing | The buyer needs a reference price or has to compare billing options | Use an actual comparable price and identify what it represents. Give alternatives the same units and billing basis. Show both the monthly equivalent and the total charged for an annual plan, alongside relevant terms and fees. |
| Social proof | Buyers need evidence that people with a similar use case found the offer useful | Show attributable reviews, a documented case study, or scoped usage figures. Keep dates, sample sizes, and the source available where they affect interpretation. Recommend plans by use case. Use popularity labels only when the underlying data support them. |
| Scarcity | Inventory, seats, or an actual deadline affect the decision | Show the quantity or expiry from the authoritative source at the point of choice. Show what happens when it runs out or expires. Use neutral availability copy when status is unknown. |
| Reciprocity | People can judge the product more easily after trying something useful | Offer a working sample, estimate, or preview before asking for a signup. Make the sample usable on its own. Explain the next step so accepting it creates no implied obligation. |
| Loss aversion | Continuing would delete work, revoke access, or otherwise cause a material loss | Name exactly what changes and when. Offer a way to save, export, undo, or cancel where the product supports it. Keep normal purchase and cancellation choices easy to find. |

Check that a buyer can explain the total cost and choose a fitting option without reading the surrounding sales pitch. When comparing variants, consider comprehension, refunds, and support questions alongside conversion. [Anchoring experiments](https://doi.org/10.1162/00335530360535153) · [Framing experiments](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683) · [Social influence field experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23929980/) · [Reciprocity experiment](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ046625) · [Loss aversion experiments](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/261737)

## Onboarding and recurring goals

| Pattern | When it helps | What to build |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IKEA effect | The product gets more useful when a person customizes a result they can finish | Start with a usable preset. Let them make one meaningful change and immediately see the finished result. Keep setup skippable and the result editable. |
| Endowed progress | Useful setup work already exists, or a loyalty program gives a genuine head start | Show imported or completed steps and say where they came from. Label a real welcome credit as such. Keep the remaining steps and threshold clear. |
| Commitment and consistency, identity-based habits | The person has chosen a goal worth returning to | Let them write or choose an achievable action and tie it to an existing cue, such as reading a page after lunch. Let them change the goal, pause, or choose a different cue. Use identity language when it reflects how they describe the goal. |
| Fresh start effect | People want to resume after a break or begin a new period | Offer a new starting point at a meaningful milestone. Keep prior work available and make it easy to continue from the previous point. |
| Loading the next trigger | A step taken now will produce an update the person wants later | Let them follow a reply, a stock update, or a saved item's change. Notify on that event. Give them control over the channel, frequency, and whether to stop following. |

For example, a setup flow can acknowledge that it imported a profile, let the person adjust one useful preset, and offer to notify them when the requested import finishes. Check completion of the useful setup task, successful resumption, and whether people can change or turn off reminders when they want to. [IKEA effect experiments](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002) · [Endowed progress study](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480) · [Implementation intention experiments](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/gollwitzer_brandstatter_1997_-_implementation_intentions_effective_goal_pursuit.pdf) · [Identity-based motivation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2009.05.008) · [Fresh start study](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901) · [Eyal's explanation of the next trigger](https://www.nirandfar.com/the-psychology-behind-why-we-cant-stop-messaging/)

## Discovery, sharing, and virtual companions

- **Curiosity gap.** In editorial or exploratory content, give a specific preview that promises something the next view delivers. Keep the information needed for a purchase or other consequential decision visible before commitment.
- **Variable reward.** Use variety when discovering something new is the point. A creative tool can offer a different template when someone asks for another idea. Keep core functionality, earned progress, and paid outcomes predictable.
- **Status and social currency.** Recognize a concrete contribution or achievement with context, such as a portfolio entry or earned credential. Offer optional sharing when there is something useful to share. Let people preview and control what becomes public.
- **Tamagotchi effect.** When caring for a virtual companion is part of the brief, let users see the consequences of care and make the interactions rewarding in themselves. Give them a pause or a straightforward way to resume with their progress intact.

Check whether the preview delivers, the shared artifact explains itself, and people can return after a break. Measure the action the user came to complete. [Uncertain incentive experiments](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/46/1/69/5050467) · [Turkle's observations on virtual pets](https://smg.media.mit.edu/library/Turkle.Diary.LondonReviewOfBooks.html)
